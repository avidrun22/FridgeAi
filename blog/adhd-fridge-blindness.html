I'm an ADHDer who kept throwing out groceries. Here's what finally worked.

By Greg, founder of ok2eat

I cleaned out my fridge last weekend and threw away $40 of produce. A bag of spinach I'd bought with real intentions on Sunday, half-liquid by Friday. A pint of strawberries I forgot existed by Tuesday. Three avocados past hope. The crisper drawer was, as my partner kindly puts it, "a graveyard."

This is not a one-time event. It is the rhythm of my kitchen. The pattern goes:

  • Sunday: grocery shopping with real intentions. Roasted vegetables this week. Smoothies. We're going to be people who eat salads.
  • Monday: motivation holds. Cook the planned dinner. Wash the spinach. Feel righteous.
  • Tuesday: tired. Order DoorDash. The spinach is fine, we'll get to it tomorrow.
  • Wednesday: forget the spinach exists.
  • Friday: remember the spinach. It is no longer spinach.
  • Sunday: grocery shopping with real intentions.

I'm not lazy. I have ADHD. The executive function it takes to plan a meal, then remember the meal exists, then assemble it on a tired Tuesday — that's three separate cognitive jobs my brain is bad at. When I read other people's stories online, I find the same pattern repeated word-for-word. "Wednesday you forget about the vegetables in the crisper drawer." That sentence comes from a meal-planning blog for ADHDers. It described my entire adult life.

Why every fridge app I tried failed

I went looking for an app to fix this. There are dozens. They all failed for the same reason: they were built for people who have the executive function to use them.

The typical flow:

  1. Open the app every time you grocery shop.
  2. Type each item in. Pick a category from a dropdown. Set the expiration date manually.
  3. Save the list.
  4. Remember to open the app again before cooking, to see what you have.

The act of opening an app to record what I bought defeats the entire point of needing the app. If I had the discipline to do that consistently, I wouldn't be losing $40 of produce a week. I'd just remember the spinach.

The other apps are fine if you're a meal-planning hobbyist who gets joy from organizing. They are not built for people whose executive function gas tank is empty by 7pm on a Tuesday.

The mindset shift: passive over active

The breakthrough wasn't a tool. It was a question: what if my brain didn't have to do any of the work?

Not "do the work but with reminders." Not "do the work but easier." Genuinely: none of the work. No typing. No category dropdowns. No remembering to open the app. The fridge tracker should run on my groceries the way Spotify runs on my listening — passively, in the background, surfacing what's relevant when it's relevant.

That reframe is what made me build my own. I'm a developer, so it was within reach. Most people would just keep losing produce.

The system that actually works for my brain

Three principles I'd recommend even if you don't end up using ok2eat:

1. Adding things should be effortless or it won't happen. The single biggest unlock for me was scanning grocery receipts. I take a photo of the receipt at checkout. The app reads it. Twelve items appear in my fridge inventory. I did nothing. Compare that to typing each item into a list app — there is no universe where Tuesday-night-me is doing that.

2. The reminder has to come to you, not the other way around. A fridge tracker that requires you to open the app is dead on arrival. I get one short email at 9am every day: "you've got 2 things expiring in 3 days, here's what they are." I don't have to remember anything. The email remembers.

3. The action and the information have to be next to each other. Knowing the spinach is going bad doesn't help if I still have to figure out what to do with it. So when I open the app to see what's expiring, the recipe ideas are right there — pulled from what I actually have in the fridge, not generic Pinterest recipes for ingredients I'd need to go buy.

These three things — passive intake, push reminders, action right next to information — are roughly half the design philosophy of ok2eat. The other half is sharing all of it with my partner so we don't both buy the same gallon of milk.

It's not perfect

I still throw produce away. Less than before, but not zero. The receipt scanner sometimes misreads a brand name. The recipe suggestions occasionally miss good ideas. ADHD doesn't get fixed by an app.

But I went from cleaning out the crisper drawer every weekend in shame to doing it about once a month with a normal level of mild irritation. My partner and I no longer buy two of the same thing. The shared shopping list is the calmest part of our domestic life. The week-on-week food spend dropped about 25% as soon as we stopped throwing things out.

If you're an ADHDer reading this, you don't need a fridge tracker. You need a fridge tracker that doesn't require executive function. That's the bar.
